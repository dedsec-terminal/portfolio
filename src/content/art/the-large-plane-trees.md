The trunks arrive before the street does. Their broad gray-blue forms divide the scene into narrow openings, and the road workers appear almost as another detail of the repair site: small figures beside heaps of stone and a line of pale curbstones. Only after looking through the trees do I find the buildings and lamppost at the left edge, which give the scene a sense of ordinary town life.

The title's road menders could suggest a picture organized around labor. Yet the workers are not placed at the center as protagonists. The trees occupy nearly the whole canvas, their branches pressing across the upper view and their roots meeting the disturbed ground. The human task feels real but temporary, held within a landscape that will remain after the road is smooth again.

Color keeps the view from settling into a simple foreground and background. Yellow and ochre leaves flicker against passages of blue, while the trunks twist in uneven strokes rather than forming a neat row. The path seems to turn away between them, but the thick paint makes even open ground feel active. I read that density as a kind of attention: the scene does not ask for a grand event, only for time spent noticing how a familiar street is made up of work, shade, and living forms.

Even the road's pale surface is interrupted by stones and marks of labor, so it never becomes a blank stage for the trees. The small scale of the people makes the street feel lived in rather than deserted, while their work gives the quiet view a present tense.

The figure in [Wanderer above the Sea of Fog](/art/wanderer-above-the-sea-of-fog) gives the landscape a place from which to look. Here the trunks keep interrupting every view of the street. The scene comes together through attention to the edges.
