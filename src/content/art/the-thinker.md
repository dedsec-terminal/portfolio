One elbow reaches across to rest on the opposite knee. The head sinks into the hand and the free arm hangs low. That crossing gives the pose its tension. The legs and shoulders seem to hold the whole body in place while its attention goes somewhere the viewer cannot follow.

The scale of the Cleveland bronze makes it possible to read that posture from a distance. The longer I look, the less the familiar hand at the chin explains on its own. The pressed lips, lifted shoulder and tightly folded knees all contribute. Thinking has a physical cost in this sculpture. Even a body at rest can show the effort of holding something in mind.

Rodin developed the figure in 1880–81 for *The Gates of Hell*. Cleveland’s object is an enlarged lifetime cast. Its broken lower edges belong to the history of this casting. The museum records that it was severely damaged in 1970. The missing feet change how the figure seems to bear its weight in the photograph, giving the massive body an unexpectedly uncertain footing. [Cleveland Museum of Art exhibition record](https://www.clevelandart.org/exhibitions/rodin-100-years)

In [The Sleep of Reason Produces Monsters](/art/the-sleep-of-reason), Goya makes inward life visible through the creatures around a sleeping figure. Rodin gives the viewer fewer clues about what occupies the mind. The visible work happens in the posture, down to the small effort of a hand holding a head.
