Most exhibitions built around the word *home* drift quickly into nostalgia—neat photographs on a wall and tidy captions telling you what to feel. What made *Geographies of Distance: Remembering Home*—the National Pavilion of India at the 61st Venice Biennale (2026), winner of the Visitors' Lion for Best National Participation—work so well is that every artwork inside the Isolotto space treated shelter as something provisional: thread holding the outline of a demolished house, bamboo tied together with rope, soil cracking as water leaves it, and fragile paper masquerading as stone.

## 1. *Permanent Address* (2026)

Walk up to *Permanent Address* and your eyes expect mass: brick courses, iron window grilles, heavy door frames. Then you realize every line is silk, cotton, and nylon thread stretched over steel frames, tracing the life-size rooms of a demolished 1952 New Delhi house as translucent membranes in a dark hall.

Because the walls are woven instead of solid, depth behaves strangely. Looking through one lattice window, you see straight into the doorway behind it and the next torn brick edge beyond that. The negative space does the work: architecture usually shuts the outside out, while embroidery here keeps only the memory of hinges, bars, and walls intact after the bricks are gone. ([Original page](https://indiainvenice.com/artist/sumakshi-singh))

## 2. *Under the Same Sky* (2026)

Seventy-four suspended elements of handwoven cotton fabric, steel, and translucent lacquer float overhead in *Under the Same Sky*, catching warm light like petals, seed pods, and ribbed leaves caught mid-drift. There are no pedestals beneath them and no visible weight holding the arrangement down.

The steel stays hidden inside the stretched cotton and golden lacquer, so every sweeping arc reads as both botanical and tautly engineered—delicate enough for light to pass through the outer edges, structured enough to hang across the dark brick hall like a suspended canopy. ([Original page](https://indiainvenice.com/artist/ranjani-shettar))

## 3. *Drift* & *Not Just for Us* (2026)

Nobody carved the cracks in this freestanding wall. Made from rural Tamil Nadu soil mixed with resin and paddy grains, the surface shrank and split as water evaporated, opening deep dark faults across an expanse of warm ochre earth.

From across the gallery it looks almost like an aerial view of parched land tilted upright. The flip side changes the story further: while the front records drying and separation (*Drift*), the reverse surface preserves tracks left by peacocks, monkeys, and snakes that crossed the wet terrain while it cured outdoors (*Not Just for Us*), turning the panel into a shared record of everything that moved across the ground. ([Original page](https://indiainvenice.com/artist/alwar-balasubramaniam))

## 4. *Chaal* (2026)

Where the surrounding works deal in thread, clay, and air, *Chaal* walks straight in as raw structure: thick bamboo poles, bent cane, woven reed mats, and hand-tied white lashings modeled on the temporary scaffolding that used to wrap building sites across Indian cities before steel rules pushed it aside.

Standing inside the lattice, there is no single tidy facade. Poles angle overhead, cane flexes under tension, and light drops through the gaps onto the floor in sharp diagonals. Scaffolding is never the finished monument—it's the creaking, repairable frame people lash together while something is being built or remade. ([Original page](https://indiainvenice.com/artist/asim-waqif))

## 5. *Echoes of Home* (2026)

Up on the mezzanine, *Echoes of Home* climbs the wall like a sun-baked Ladakhi hillside settlement—flat-roofed earthen houses stacked in tiers with recessed balconies, narrow vertical vents, and dark-framed windows built to hold warmth against mountain cold.

Step closer and the apparent weight disappears: the walls are made from recycled cardboard, papier-mâché, and clay mixed with natural binders. Seeing fragile paper masonry modeled after high-altitude Himalayan homes inside a Venetian lagoon hall brings two geographies together quietly—both shaped by water, erosion, and the narrow margins within which shelter survives. ([Original page](https://indiainvenice.com/artist/skarma-sonam-tashi))
