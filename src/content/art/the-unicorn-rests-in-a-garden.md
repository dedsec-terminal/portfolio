The unicorn sits calmly inside a low circular wooden fence. A slender pomegranate tree rises directly behind it, laden with heavy red fruit. Small drops of pomegranate juice touch the animal's pale coat. A golden collar around its neck connects by a loose chain to the tree trunk. Outside the wooden pale, a dense field of dark green millefleurs spreads across the woven surface, packed with carefully depicted flowers.

The physical details raise questions about confinement and peace. The wooden rails remain low enough for the creature to step across with ease. The chain around the tree stays slack. Medieval folklore celebrated the unicorn as an untamable creature of immense strength. In this garden, the animal rests with calm alertness. The enclosure functions as a defined sanctuary, where boundaries offer protection. The animal appears to remain by its own quiet inclination.

In [Island of the Dead](/art/island-of-the-dead), steep rock walls and dark cypresses create a sheltered space where grief finds stillness. This tapestry approaches containment with a similar gentleness. The fence marks a border between the dense forest life and a peaceful center. Decoration and structure combine to frame safety.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art dates the French and South Netherlandish weaving to 1495–1505, cataloging it as object 467642, accession 37.80.6, from the 1937 gift of John D. Rockefeller Jr. under Open Access. Historians continue to study whether the scene represents an emblem of courtly devotion, an allegory of sacred mystery, or a celebration of earthly renewal. The open fence leaves those symbolic paths alive for every viewer who stands before it.
