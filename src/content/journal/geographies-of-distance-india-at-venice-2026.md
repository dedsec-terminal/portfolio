Most exhibitions built around the word *home* drift pretty quickly into nostalgia—neat photographs on a wall, warm lighting, and a wall text telling you how much you ought to feel. What made *Geographies of Distance: Remembering Home*—the India Pavilion at the 61st Venice Biennale (2026), which just won the Visitors' Lion for Best National Participation—so striking is that almost every artwork inside the Isolotto space at the Arsenale treated *home* as something unstable: a surface cracking, a structure tied together with rope, or an outline made of thread after the bricks are already gone.

What follows is a closer look at the five works themselves, and why putting those specific materials in the same room worked so well.

## Permanent Address (Sumakshi Singh, 2026)

On paper, rebuilding a demolished 1952 New Delhi house at life size out of white silk, cotton, and nylon thread sounds like a delicate craft exercise. In space, *Permanent Address* behaves more like an apparition. Walls, hinges, and brickwork appear as translucent, lace-like membranes stretched over steel frames—solid enough to walk through, thin enough that the dark room behind them keeps bleeding through.

The trick isn't realism; it's what the gaps in the weave do to your sense of shelter. You expect a house to block the air and hold weight. Here, the house only exists because someone stitched the memory of its geometry back together after the physical building vanished. It sits close to the quiet tension in [The Unicorn Rests in a Garden](/art/the-unicorn-rests-in-a-garden): an enclosure whose boundary barely looks strong enough to hold anything, yet defines the whole emotional center of the room.

## Under the Same Sky (Ranjani Shettar, 2026)

Look up in the pavilion and *Under the Same Sky* fills the air with 74 suspended sculptural elements built from handwoven cotton fabric, steel, and lacquer. Lit from within and against the brick of the Arsenale, the forms look halfway between unfurling leaves, pods, and deep-sea flora—weightless enough that you half expect them to drift if a door opens.

What keeps the piece from feeling purely decorative is the tension in how each element hangs. Suspension isn't just a display choice here; it's the structural logic of the work. Every curve balances gravity against steel and tensioned fabric, like an acoustic chord held just above eye level while heavier earthbound works sit underneath it.

## Drift & Not Just for Us (Alwar Balasubramaniam, 2026)

If *Permanent Address* turns memory into air and thread, *Drift* and *Not Just for Us* bring you straight back down to dry ground. Built from soil sourced in rural Tamil Nadu mixed with resin and paddy grains, the wall panels aren't painted to look cracked—they *are* cracked, shaped by water evaporating out of clay until fissures open across the surface.

The detail that stays with you is what happens on the reverse side of the wall: while the front traces the geometric split of drying earth, the broader terrain preserves animal tracks—peacocks, monkeys, snakes—that crossed the wet surface while it cured outdoors. Instead of treating landscape as scenery you look at from a safe distance, the work literally records who else shares the ground.

## Chaal (Asim Waqif, 2026)

Right in the middle of those fragile threads and suspended leaves sits *Chaal*: a towering lattice of bamboo, cane, reed weaving, and hand-tied lashings modeled on the bamboo scaffolding that used to wrap every building site across Indian cities before steel and aluminum codes pushed it aside.

Compared to the quietness around it, *Chaal* is unapologetically structural and provisional. Scaffolding isn't the finished building—it's the temporary skeleton people climb while something else is being raised or torn down. Placing it inside a historic Venetian arsenal turns the whole question of "home" around: maybe home isn't the permanent monument at the end of construction, but the creaking, repairable frame you keep re-lashing while things change around you.

## Echoes of Home (Skarma Sonam Tashi, 2026)

Up on the mezzanine, *Echoes of Home* gathers a dense hillside settlement of miniature Ladakhi earth-walled houses built out of papier-mâché, recycled cardboard, and clay mixed with natural binders. Grouped tightly across a slope, with deep recessed windows and stepped flat roofs, the model reads from afar like stone masonry—until you notice the fragile, fibrous paper skin holding it together.

Putting high-altitude Himalayan earthen homes—built to trap winter heat and breathe with glacial water cycles—inside a lagoon city negotiating rising tides makes the juxtaposition do the talking without a lecture. Neither Ladakh nor Venice gets to conquer its terrain; both survive by adapting to water, weight, and limits.

---

Seeing these five installations together (even from documentation and the [India in Venice 2026 archive](https://indiainvenice.com/)) explains why half a million visitors connected with the pavilion. Nothing in the room pretends that distance is tidy or that shelter lasts forever—and yet none of the pieces collapse into despair either. They just pay close attention to what thread, bamboo, soil, cotton, and paper can still hold onto.
